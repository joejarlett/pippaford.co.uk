# Voice guideline

How to write for pippaford.co.uk. Use this for any new page or edit.

There is a separate document in the KB — _Pipper — Voicing Guide for Content Creation_ —
drawn from a voice memo about facilitating a peer group. That one describes how Pippa
speaks **to colleagues**. This one describes how the site speaks **to prospective
clients**. Where they conflict, this one wins, because the reader is different.

---

## Who is reading

Assume the least experienced reader, not the most informed one:

- **Has probably never had therapy.** Does not know what a first session involves, what
  it costs, how long it lasts, or whether what they are dealing with is "enough" to
  warrant it. Many are quietly worried they are wasting a professional's time.
- **Has not heard of ecotherapy**, and certainly not eco-depth psychology. May not know
  dramatherapy is a regulated profession rather than an activity.
- **Is often anxious while reading.** They may be deciding, in a couple of minutes,
  whether to email a stranger about the worst thing in their life.
- **May be a parent** looking for help for a child rather than for themselves.
- **A minority are practitioners** looking for supervision. They can handle more
  technical language — but only the `/supervision` page should assume it.

The reader is not evaluating Pippa's credentials against other therapists. They are
asking two things: _does this person understand something like my situation_, and _is it
safe to make contact_.

---

## The two things to hold together

Everything on the site has to be **approachable** and **clinically grounded** at once.
These pull in opposite directions, and most bad therapy copy fails by choosing one:

- Too warm alone → reads as vague, unqualified, possibly a bit woo. The reader cannot
  tell whether this is a regulated professional or an enthusiast.
- Too clinical alone → reads as cold and institutional. The reader cannot tell whether
  they would be treated as a person.

**The resolution is nearly always sequencing, not compromise.** State the clinical fact
plainly, then immediately translate it into what it means for the reader. Do not water
down the fact, and do not leave it untranslated.

> Dramatherapy is an allied health profession, regulated in the United Kingdom by the
> Health and Care Professions Council. **_It is the deliberate clinical application of the
> therapeutic properties of drama and theatre…_**

The credential establishes seriousness; the sentence after it does the explaining.

## Rules

**Plain word first, technical term second.** Explain the thing, then name it — so the
term lands as a label for something already understood rather than a barrier to entry.

> …an adult who cannot find words for grief will find an image for it, and then the image
> does the talking. **_Therapists call that aesthetic distance._**

**Concrete beats abstract, always.** Abstraction is where an unfamiliar reader gives up.
If a paragraph defines one abstract noun using two more, rewrite it around something the
reader can picture.

**Write to "you".** Not "the client", not "someone", not "a person". Third person adds
distance exactly where warmth is needed. Be consistent within a page.

**Active voice, and name who is doing the thing.** "They are usually told they are
catastrophising" is better than "it is often dismissed as catastrophising" — it is more
direct, and it validates rather than floats free.

**State things; do not hedge.** "It can retraumatise" beats "it might be worth
considering that it could be counterproductive". Hedging reads as evasion to an anxious
reader. Where genuine uncertainty exists, name it plainly instead: "each person's journey
is different".

**Answer the reader's real questions.** What happens in a session, will this suit me,
what if I get upset, who will know, what if it is not working. These matter more than
modality descriptions and are still under-covered. (Fees and session length are the
exception — see Decisions.)

**Say what something is not.** Pre-empting misconceptions is high value here: "it is not
a yoga class", "no artistic ability is required", "it has nothing to do with being
outdoorsy". Each removes a reason not to make contact.

**Sentences may run long, then land short.** The rhythm carries the warmth. A short
declarative after a long sentence is where conviction lives.

**British English.** Realise, recognised, behaviour, practise (verb) / practice (noun).

## Register by page

Not every page wants the same balance.

| Page          | Weighting              | Why                                                 |
| ------------- | ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Home          | Warm, minimal          | First impression; the job is to invite, not explain |
| About         | Warmest                | This is where trust is decided                      |
| Approach      | Most clinical          | The reader is asking "is this person serious"       |
| Therapy pages | Balanced               | Explaining a method _and_ reassuring                |
| Supervision   | Technical              | Audience is other practitioners                     |
| Contact       | Warm, plain, practical | Lower the barrier to writing the email              |

The **trauma-informed** material carries the least playfulness anywhere. Someone is
likely reading it because something happened to them; wit there reads as not taking it
seriously. Warmth yes, lightness no.

## Avoid

- **Jargon left untranslated.** "Depth-oriented frameworks", "relational stance",
  "psychodynamic" — all fine to use, none fine to leave standing alone.
- **Sounding like an NHS policy document.** If a sentence could appear in one, rewrite it.
- **Spiritual-adjacent language without grounding.** "Nature as co-therapist", "ecological
  consciousness", "nature-based wisdom" are the phrases most likely to lose a sceptical
  reader. Keep them only where grounded in the same breath.
- **Bulleted lists standing in for prose**, and prose that is a bulleted list in disguise
  ("This means several things. That… That… That…").
- **Defensiveness.** "Both are legitimate" answers an objection the reader has not raised.
- **Swearing.** The peer-group guide says not to sanitise it out; that is right for
  colleagues and wrong here. The reader cannot calibrate back, and some will be parents or
  in distress. Deliver the same conviction through syntax instead — flat contradiction,
  short declaratives, naming harm without softening it.
- **Promising outcomes.** Describe what the work involves, not what it will achieve.
  Both a clinical and a regulatory line.

## Two checks before publishing

1. **The stranger test.** Would someone who has never had therapy, and has never heard of
   any of these modalities, finish this paragraph knowing what would actually happen to
   them? If not, it is not ready.
2. **The accountability check.** Every claim about training, registration, evidence base
   or what a session involves must be true and approved by Pippa. She is professionally
   accountable for everything on this site.

## Decisions

Settled with Joe, August 2026. Treat these as binding unless revisited.

### The reader is an adult referring themselves

Write for someone seeking therapy for themselves, most likely for the first time.
Everything else is secondary and gets its own signposted place: parents on the FAQ and
the "who I work with" section, practitioners on `/supervision`. When a sentence would
serve two readers less well than it serves one, serve the self-referring adult.

### Name distress in the FAQ, not in the body copy

Main pages describe **the work**, not the person reading. Do not open a page by telling
someone what they are probably feeling — it presumes, and a reader who does not recognise
themselves feels described rather than met.

The concrete situations live in two places instead: the FAQ, and the "Often helpful for"
lists on each therapy page. That is where a reader goes looking to check whether they
qualify, so that is where recognition belongs.

> Body copy: "Therapy offers a regular, confidential hour to think about what is happening
> and why, at a pace you set."
>
> "Often helpful for": "Anxiety, low mood and periods of feeling stuck."

### Spiritual language: keep what is genuinely hers, always glossed

Depth, Jungian, ritual, threshold, the ecological self — these reflect how Pippa actually
works and stripping them out would misrepresent the practice. Keep them.

But never leave one standing alone. Pair every such term with a plain-language gloss in
the same breath, so a sceptical reader has something solid to hold:

> …ritual work to mark a transition
>
> The natural world is treated as a genuine participant in the therapy, **not a pleasant
> setting for it.**

The test is not whether a phrase sounds spiritual. It is whether a reader who does not
share that frame can still tell what would actually happen in a session.

### Fees and session length stay off the site

Both are handled in the enquiry conversation, not published. Do not add a fees page, and
do not state a price or a session length in copy.

This is a deliberate trade and worth naming: it is the most-searched missing information
on any therapist site, and its absence does cost enquiries. Since the site cannot answer
the question, it must make **asking** feel easy and unembarrassing — which puts more
weight on `/contact`. Make plain that a first email can be two lines, that questions about
cost are welcome and normal, and roughly when to expect a reply.
